SECTION 1: AMAZON LEADERSHIP PRINCIPLES
========================================================================

Amazon's 14 Leadership Principles define company culture and drive interview evaluation. These aren't just words—they shape every decision.

CUSTOMER OBSESSION
Leaders start with customer needs and work backwards. Every technical decision should answer: "Does this improve customer experience?" Not "Is this technically elegant?"

In interviews, demonstrate customer focus. When solving a problem, consider customer impact. Would your solution handle the customer's actual workflow or just the happy path?

For system design, prioritize availability and usability over theoretical purity. A 99% reliable system serving customers is better than 100% perfect but unavailable.

For engineering managers: advocate for customers internally. Push back on roadmap items that don't serve customers. Your team should understand customer context.

OWNERSHIP
Take full responsibility for outcomes. Don't blame other teams, tools, or circumstances. "Own it" means seeing through to the end—not just writing code but shipping, monitoring, supporting.

Owned problems are solved faster and better because you're invested. When something fails, ask "What could I have done differently?" not "Why did someone else cause this?"

In interviews, show ownership: ask clarifying questions about failure, take responsibility for edge cases, think about monitoring and alerting, not just building.

INVENT AND SIMPLIFY
Encourage innovation and simplification simultaneously. Complex solutions are debt. Seek simplicity but challenge status quo when needed.

For engineers: if something is hard, maybe it shouldn't be. Can we simplify the problem? Can we simplify the solution? Some of the best inventions are the simplest.

Process inefficiency is technical debt too. If your deploy takes 2 hours, that's a problem worth solving.

LEARN AND BE CURIOUS
Growth mindset is mandatory. Technologies change. Industries shift. Stay curious and humble.

In interviews, be honest about what you don't know but show willingness to learn. Ask good questions. Show curiosity about the problem domain.

BIAS FOR ACTION
Analysis paralysis kills. Once you understand the problem well enough, move forward. Iteration beats perfection.

This doesn't mean reckless. It means: make good decisions quickly, ship, gather data, adjust. "Two-way doors" (reversible decisions) should be made fast. "One-way doors" require more analysis.

For leaders: empower teams to make decisions. Create blameless failure culture. People who bias for action sometimes fail; that's acceptable cost of speed.

DELIVER RESULTS
Ultimately, results matter. A perfect process that doesn't ship is worth zero. Ship first, perfect later.

Ownership combines with this principle: you're responsible for outcomes, so do whatever is needed to deliver them.

DIG DEEP
Understand root causes, not symptoms. Push further when something doesn't make sense. Challenge assumptions including your own.

Common interview mistake: solving the stated problem without validating assumptions. Dig deeper. Why does customer need this? What are they really trying to accomplish?

HAVE BACKBONE; DISAGREE AND COMMIT
Speak up when you disagree. Challenge decisions and people respectfully. But once decision is made, commit fully.

Not "I told you so" later. Not passive aggressive compliance. Full buy-in even if you disagreed.

This builds healthy teams. Debate is encouraged. Hierarchy doesn't suppress good ideas.

THINK BIG
Start with ambitious goals. Many problems seem impossible until someone tackles them. Don't limit yourself to "reasonable" constraints.

Amazon started selling books online—huge shift from retail. AWS entered mature hosting market with inferior technology but lower costs.

Think big but start simple: break big problem into manageable pieces.

FRUGALITY
Spend money as if it's your own. Waste is enemy of profit. But frugality isn't cheapness—it's efficiency.

At scale, small optimizations compound massively. If you reduce API call latency by 10ms serving billions of requests, you save millions in infrastructure.

EARN TRUST
Trust is built through consistent delivery and honesty. Admit mistakes quickly. Take feedback well.

Avoid "resume-driven development"—building things for personal brand rather than team needs. Earn trust through serving team and customers.

SECTION 2: AWS CORE SERVICES FOR SCALABLE SYSTEMS

AMAZON S3: OBJECT STORAGE AT SCALE

S3 is Amazon's foundational service—object storage supporting 99.999999999% durability (11 9s). Billions of objects stored.

Fundamentals:
- Objects: immutable blobs with metadata, stored in buckets
- Buckets: namespaced globally, must be unique across all AWS accounts
- Keys: hierarchical paths identifying objects (e.g., photos/2024/img.jpg)
- Versioning: optional, maintains history of objects
- Lifecycle policies: automatically transition to cheaper storage or delete

Durability achieved through geographic redundancy. Objects replicated across facilities within region. Data replication is asynchronous, typically milliseconds.

Performance considerations:
- Requests eventually consistent for new objects (read-after-write)
- Existing object updates eventually consistent (might read stale version)
- Partitioning via key prefixes: put-heavy workloads benefit from random prefixes to distribute across partitions
- Transfer acceleration uses CloudFront for faster uploads
- Multipart upload enables parallel transfers for large objects

Access patterns:
- GET/PUT are core operations, milliseconds latency for small objects
- List operations expensive—index in separate systems if frequent access needed
- Presigned URLs grant temporary access without AWS credentials
- Cross-origin resource sharing (CORS) enables browser access

Cost: cheapest for infrequent access. Intelligent-tiering automates transitions based on access patterns. Glacier archival for cold data.

S3 powers: websites (static hosting), data lakes (analytics), backups, CDN origin (CloudFront).

DYNAMODB: SERVERLESS DISTRIBUTED DATABASE

DynamoDB eliminates database operational burden. No servers to manage, scales automatically, pays only for throughput and storage.

Data Model:
- Tables: collections of items, must have partition key
- Partition key: determines which partition stores item, must be unique in table
- Sort key: optional, enables range queries within partition
- Attributes: schema-less, items can have different attributes
- Global Secondary Index (GSI): alternate key with same/different partition key

Throughput:
- Provisioned: specify read/write capacity units, pay for capacity
- On-demand: pay per request, spiky workloads
- Read capacity unit: 4KB strongly consistent read
- Write capacity unit: 1KB write
- Provisioned mode cheaper for predictable workloads, on-demand better for unpredictable

Consistency:
- Strong consistency: reads latest value, higher latency, consumes 2x read capacity
- Eventually consistent: may read older value, lower latency, normal capacity
- Choose based on use case

Query patterns:
- GetItem: retrieve item by key, O(1)
- Query: retrieve range of items with same partition key, efficient with sort key
- Scan: full table scan, expensive on large tables
- Batch operations: more efficient than individual requests

Partitioning internally: items with same partition key go to same partition. Partition automatically splits if exceeds capacity. Partition key should distribute evenly to avoid hot partitions.

Common mistakes:
- Bad partition key: all items with same partition key becomes single partition, bottleneck
- Using scan instead of query: linear time instead of logarithmic
- Strong consistency everywhere: unnecessary latency and cost
- No indexes: queries must scan
- Not monitoring consumed capacity: billing surprises

DynamoDB powers: real-time applications, mobile backends, serverless architectures, sessions.

LAMBDA: SERVERLESS COMPUTE

Lambda runs code without managing servers. Pay only for execution time. Ideal for event-driven workloads and APIs.

Model:
- Functions: code + runtime + configuration
- Triggers: events that invoke function (S3 upload, DynamoDB stream, HTTP request, scheduled)
- Execution environment: spun up on demand, reused for subsequent invocations
- Cold start: first invocation has latency for environment setup (~100ms), warm start is fast

Architecture:
- Event-driven: Lambda ideal for responding to events
- API Gateway integration: run web services without servers
- Microservices: each Lambda function is separate service
- Async: invoke function without waiting for result

Constraints:
- Maximum execution time: 15 minutes
- Memory: 128MB to 10GB (CPU scales with memory)
- Package size: 50MB zipped, 250MB unzipped
- Stateless: each invocation fresh environment (reuse across invocations for performance)

Optimization:
- Connection pooling: reuse database connections across invocations
- Provisioned concurrency: keep Lambda warm, eliminates cold starts
- Lambda@Edge: run near CloudFront edge locations
- Layers: shared code/dependencies across functions

Cold start optimization:
- Keep functions small
- Use efficient languages (Go < Node < Python < Java)
- Package only necessary dependencies
- Minimize initialization code in handler
- Use provisioned concurrency for critical paths

KINESIS: REAL-TIME DATA STREAMING

Kinesis ingests and processes streaming data at scale. Millions of records per second, millisecond latency.

Architecture:
- Streams: sequences of data records, partitioned into shards
- Shards: parallel data channels, each processes independent data
- Records: contain sequence number, partition key, data blob
- Retention: configurable 24 hours to 365 days

Throughput:
- Each shard: 1MB/sec input or 1000 records/sec input
- Scale by adding shards
- Auto-scaling: adjust shards based on metrics

Use cases:
- Real-time analytics: aggregate metrics as events arrive
- Application logs: capture and process application events
- IoT sensor data: ingest millions of sensors
- Event sources: feed events to consumers (Lambda, EC2, Redshift)

Consumer patterns:
- Lambda integration: trigger Lambda on batch of records
- Kinesis Analytics: SQL queries on streaming data
- Custom consumers: poll records directly, process, checkpoint position

Partition key determines shard: records with same key go to same shard, enabling ordering. Different keys distribute across shards.

Fan-out patterns:
- Standard: shared throughput across consumers, each must poll
- Enhanced: dedicated throughput per consumer, lower latency

Exactly-once processing requires idempotency. Duplicate records possible; application must handle.

SECTION 3: DYNAMODB DEEP DIVE: DISTRIBUTED KEY-VALUE MASTERY
========================================================================

PARTITION KEY DESIGN: THE FOUNDATION

Partition key design determines whether DynamoDB can scale elastically or becomes bottleneck. Poor choice is often fatal.

Characteristics of good partition keys:
- Evenly distributed across values: if 80% items have same partition key, 80% traffic hits 1 partition
- High cardinality: many distinct values, enables fine-grained partitioning
- Access-pattern aligned: most queries use partition key to narrow down data

Examples:
- User ID for user-specific data: high cardinality, naturally distributed
- Product ID for inventory: good if requests distributed across products
- Timestamp alone: bad, all recent data on single partition (hot partition)
- User ID + date: good, enables time-based partitioning and historical queries

Hot partitions occur when partition key distributes unevenly. Solutions:
- Add random suffix to key: put{userId}#{random} creates multiple partitions per user
- Weighted random suffix: more traffic goes to more partitions
- Separate table for hot data: use separate DynamoDB table with different partition key

QUERY OPTIMIZATION: USING INDEXES EFFECTIVELY

Query returns items with matching partition key, optionally filtered by sort key range.

Query is efficient if:
- Partition key is in query (narrows to single partition)
- Sort key range is narrow (fewer items to examine)

Scan examines all items in table—linear time, expensive. Use scan only when necessary (analytics, migrations).

Global Secondary Index: alternate query interface:
- Different partition key: enables querying by different attribute
- Different sort key: enables different sort orders
- Consumes separate provisioned capacity
- Eventual consistency (updated asynchronously)
- Trade-off: extra storage and capacity cost, but enables new query patterns

Example: user data with user_id partition key and email sort key. Need to look up users by email. Create GSI with email as partition key. Now email lookups are efficient.

Local Secondary Index: same partition key, different sort key:
- Limited to 10GB per partition key value
- Strong consistency option available
- Consumes capacity from base table
- Use sparingly, not for unlimited growth per partition key

WRITE PATTERNS AND CONDITIONAL UPDATES

Put: unconditional write, replaces entire item. Fast, simple.

Update: modify specific attributes. Partial update, attributes not mentioned unchanged.

Conditional writes: only write if condition met.
- UpdateExpression: specifies attributes to modify
- ConditionExpression: must evaluate true
- Example: "set #a = :v if #a <> :old_v" — conditional update

Optimistic locking: store version number, conditionally update only if version matches.
```
Get item: version = 5
Modify locally
UpdateItem with condition: version == 5
If concurrent update happened, condition fails (version is 6)
Retry logic handles conflict
```

Atomic counters: update attributes atomically.
```
UpdateExpression: "set #count = #count + :inc"
Thread-safe counter without separate get-then-update
```

Batch writes: PutItem + DeleteItem together, up to 25 items. More efficient than individual writes.

SECTION 4: SCALING AND OPERATIONAL EXCELLENCE

DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM PATTERNS AT AMAZON

Availability vs Consistency:
Amazon prioritizes availability. Better to serve stale data than error. DynamoDB eventually consistent by default.

Idempotency: operations produce same result regardless of repetition. Critical for distributed systems where retries happen.

Implement via idempotency key: client provides unique ID for operation. Server checks if already processed, returns cached result if yes.

Timeouts: always have timeouts on external calls. Don't wait indefinitely. Cascading timeouts: caller timeout > callee timeout.

Retry logic: exponential backoff avoids thundering herd. Retry transient failures, don't retry 4xx errors.

Circuit breaker: failing service, open circuit, fail fast, then half-open to test recovery.

CACHING STRATEGIES

ElastiCache (Redis/Memcached):
- In-memory cache for frequently accessed data
- Redis: richer data types, persistence option
- Memcached: simpler, purely in-memory
- Reduces database load, lower latency

Cache-aside pattern: check cache, on miss fetch from database, populate cache.

Cache invalidation: hardest problem. TTL-based (automatic expiration), event-based (invalidate on update), or both.

Cache warming: pre-populate with common data on startup.

MONITORING AND OBSERVABILITY

CloudWatch: metrics and logs. Track:
- Application metrics: response time, throughput, errors
- Infrastructure: CPU, memory, network
- Application logs: debug issues

Alarms: alert when metrics exceed thresholds. Trigger auto-scaling or notifications.

X-Ray: distributed tracing. Track request through all services, identify latency bottlenecks.

Metrics to monitor:
- Latency: p50, p99, p99.9 (tail latency matters most)
- Throughput: requests/sec
- Error rate: failures / total requests
- Saturation: how full is resource

Alert on anomalies: compare current to baseline, alert if significant change.

SECTION 5: SYSTEM DESIGN AT AMAZON SCALE
========================================================================

DESIGNING SYSTEMS FOR MILLIONS OF REQUESTS

Handle traffic spikes:
- Auto-scaling: adjust capacity based on metrics
- Circuit breaker: fail fast under overload instead of cascading failures
- Rate limiting: throttle clients to prevent overload
- Graceful degradation: drop non-critical features before core fails

Data consistency:
- Strong consistency: ACID transactions, simpler but harder to scale
- Eventual consistency: eventually all replicas agree, high availability
- Choose consistency level per use case, not global

Caching layers:
- Database caching: query results in Redis
- API response caching: cache entire responses
- Client-side caching: browsers cache static assets
- Each layer reduces load on layers below

RESILIENCE PATTERNS

Bulkheads: isolate failure. Different thread pools for different operations. One slow operation doesn't starve others.

Retry storms: if client retries too aggressively, compound failures. Use exponential backoff and jitter.

Degraded mode: instead of 503 (service unavailable), serve reduced-quality responses. Stale data beats no data.

Mutation and Query separation: different services for reads and writes. Scale independently.

SECTION 8: ADVANCED AWS SERVICES FOR INTERVIEW

REDSHIFT: DATA WAREHOUSE AT SCALE

Redshift is Amazon's data warehouse for petabyte-scale analytics. Competes with Snowflake, BigQuery.

Architecture:
- Cluster of nodes running in parallel
- Columnar storage: optimize for analytics (reading specific columns vs all columns)
- Massive parallel processing (MPP): query distributed across all nodes
- Compression: store more data in less space

Use cases: historical data analysis, business intelligence, reporting.

Queries: SQL, familiar to data analysts. Pushes complexity to infrastructure.

Optimization: distribution keys and sort keys critical for performance. Poor choice causes slow queries.

SQS: QUEUE SERVICE

Amazon SQS provides message queues for decoupling producer from consumer.

Standard queues: unordered, at-least-once delivery, fast.

FIFO queues: ordered (first-in-first-out), exactly-once delivery, slower but consistent.

Messages: up to 256KB text. Larger data stored in S3, reference passed in message.

Visibility timeout: message invisible to other consumers for period after retrieval. Prevent multiple processing.

Dead letter queues: messages exceeding retry limit sent to DLQ for analysis.

Applications: job queues, email delivery, background processing.

ELASTICACHE ADVANCED PATTERNS

Session storage: store user sessions in Redis instead of database.

Leaderboards: sorted sets in Redis perfect for gaming/competitive apps.

Rate limiting: use Redis INCR to count requests, reset periodically.

Pub/Sub: real-time message streaming within application.

Geo indexing: store geographic data, query by radius.

SECTION 10: ALGORITHMIC FOUNDATIONS FOR BACKEND ENGINEERS
========================================================================

RATE LIMITING ALGORITHMS

Token bucket: tokens accumulate at fixed rate, request consumes token. No tokens, reject.

Leaky bucket: requests queue, leak at constant rate, queue overflows on too many requests.

Sliding window: count requests in rolling time window, reject if exceed threshold.

Distributed rate limiting: rate limiter must be centralized or synchronized across servers. Use Redis for shared counter.

CONSENSUS ALGORITHMS

Paxos: complex protocol ensuring consensus in distributed system despite failures. Academic foundation for practical systems.

Raft: simpler Paxos alternative. Easier to understand and implement. Used in etcd, Consul.

Properties: liveness (system makes progress), safety (never violates invariants), persistence (decisions survive failures).

Application: leader election, distributed locks, coordination.

DATA REPLICATION STRATEGIES

Synchronous replication: write confirmed only after replicating to N replicas. Strong consistency, higher latency.

Asynchronous replication: write confirmed immediately, replication happens background. Lower latency, weaker consistency.

Semi-synchronous: write confirmed after replicating to at least one replica. Compromise between consistency and latency.

Applications: databases, caches, message brokers.

THE FRUGALITY PRINCIPLE IN DETAIL

Frugality isn't cheapness—it's efficiency and waste elimination.

Examples:

- Reduce database query time by 5%, serve 10x more users without adding hardware

BIAS FOR ACTION PHILOSOPHY

Two-way door vs one-way door decisions:
- Two-way door: can be reversed. Take action, gather data, adjust. Wrong decision is learning opportunity, not catastrophe.
- One-way door: irreversible or very costly to reverse. Requires analysis, deliberation, higher consensus.

Most decisions are two-way. Recognize them, act quickly.

ONE-WAY DOOR DECISIONS

Strategic choice: which markets to enter, core platform architecture. Require deep analysis.

Customer trust decisions: privacy choices, data handling. Wrong choice damages trust irreparably.

Organizational structure: impacts long-term dynamics, hard to change.

Major capital investments: once committed, hard to reverse.

OPERATIONAL METRICS

Available: is service running?

Latency: how fast?

Throughput: how many requests?

Error rate: how many failures?

Cost: expenses?

Each metric tracked, graphed, alerted on.

INCIDENT RESPONSE

Declare incident when threshold violated.

Assign roles:
- Incident commander: coordinates response
- Technical lead: investigates root cause
- Communicator: updates stakeholders

Immediate actions:
- Mitigate impact (failover, reduce load)
- Investigate root cause (logs, metrics, diagnostics)
- Implement fix
- Verify resolution

Postmortem: document what happened, why it happened, how to prevent recurrence.

No blame culture: focus on systems and processes, not individual blame.

For each principle, prepare stories:
- Customer Obsession: time you prioritized customer
- Ownership: took responsibility end-to-end
- Invent and Simplify: created something or simplified process
- Learn and Be Curious: picked up new skill
- Bias for Action: made quick decision
- Deliver Results: achieved goal
- Dig Deep: investigated root cause
- Have Backbone: disagreed respectfully
- Think Big: ambitious goal
- Frugality: optimized resource use
- Earn Trust: built confidence through actions
- Are Right, A Lot: made good decision
- Hire and Develop Best: improved team

Practice telling stories concisely (2-3 minutes each).

========================================================================
CONCLUSION: AMAZON INTERVIEW MASTERY
========================================================================

Excel at Amazon interviews by:

1. Demonstrating leadership principles: customer obsession, ownership, bias for action
2. Knowing AWS: understand services, when to use each, trade-offs
3. System design thinking: scale, reliability, cost, tradeoffs
4. Communication: explain decisions, defend choices, admit unknowns
5. Problem-solving: break complex problems into pieces, iterate, test
6. Culture fit: show curiosity, humility, willingness to learn

Amazon looks for "bar raisers"—people who elevate team quality. Your interview itself raises or lowers the bar for future candidates. Make it count.

Be prepared for multiple levels of technical difficulty. Amazon interviews engineers at all levels, and each level has different expectations. Staff engineers must handle more complex scenarios, principal engineers even more so.

Finally, remember Amazon's scale. Your optimizations might affect billions of customers. Your architectural decisions might impact millions of dollars in infrastructure costs. Interviewer wants to see you thinking at this scale, making trade-offs with these implications in mind.

Demonstrate both depth and breadth: deep knowledge in specific domain plus ability to learn and adapt across technologies.
